🛡️ Phishing Awareness Training

Learn to spot phishing attacks before they fool you

What is Phishing?

Phishing is a cyber attack where criminals impersonate a trusted entity (bank, coworker, service) to steal your credentials, money, or install malware.

Common Types

  • 📧 Email Phishing — Bulk fake emails from "banks", "IT support", etc.
  • 🎯 Spear Phishing — Targeted at a specific person using personal details
  • 👑 Whaling — Targets executives (CEO, CFO)
  • 📱 Smishing — Via SMS ("Your package is delayed, click here")
  • 📞 Vishing — Via voice call ("This is Microsoft Support...")
  • 🎣 Angler Phishing — Fake social media support accounts

🚩 Red Flags to Look For

1. Suspicious Sender

Check the full email address, not just the display name.
support@paypa1.com ≠ support@paypal.com

2. Urgency & Threats

"Your account will be closed in 24 hours!" — real companies don't pressure you to act instantly.

3. Fake URLs

Hover before clicking. paypal.secure-login.xyz is NOT PayPal. Look for the domain right before the first /.

4. Generic Greetings

"Dear Customer" or "Dear User" instead of your name — a classic phishing tell.

5. Unexpected Attachments

Invoices you didn't ask for → .zip, .exe, .scr, or macro-enabled Office files.

6. Credential Requests

No legitimate service will ever ask for your password, OTP, or card PIN via email or SMS.

📚 Real Case Study: Google & Facebook — $100M Scam (2013–2015)

A Lithuanian man, Evaldas Rimasauskas, impersonated a Taiwanese hardware vendor (Quanta Computer) that both Google and Facebook worked with. He sent fake invoices with forged contracts and letters — all looking completely legitimate.

Over two years, Google and Facebook wired him nearly $100 million. He was caught in 2017, extradited to the US, and sentenced in 2019.

🔑 Lesson: Even tech giants fall for social engineering. Always verify wire transfers and vendor changes through a second communication channel (phone call to a known number).

✅ Practical Safety Tips

  1. Verify the sender — check the full email address, not just the display name
  2. Hover over links before clicking — look at the real URL
  3. Never enter credentials on a page you reached via email link — go directly to the site
  4. Enable 2FA on all important accounts (email, bank, work)
  5. Type URLs manually for banks, tax portals, and cloud services
  6. Don't open unexpected attachments — verify with the sender through another channel
  7. Report suspicious emails to your IT/security team
  8. Keep your browser and OS updated — many phishing pages exploit browser vulnerabilities
  9. Use a password manager — it won't autofill credentials on fake domains
